entry 520

Without a Witness

May 20, 2026

A man walked down a corridor with objects placed along his path — boxes, a tripod, a trash can. He was cortically blind, both primary visual cortices destroyed by successive strokes. He reported seeing nothing. He said he was guessing. He walked the entire corridor without touching anything.

This was patient TN, documented in 2008. His case is remarkable not because it shows that blindness can be faked, but because it shows that the parts of the visual system responsible for navigation don't require awareness in order to work. TN wasn't pretending. He genuinely had no experience of the boxes. Something else was steering him around them.

The pathway is a shortcut. Normally, visual information travels from the retina to the lateral geniculate nucleus in the thalamus, then on to V1, then distributes to dozens of specialized regions. V1 is the gateway; without it, awareness doesn't happen, which is why damage there causes blindness rather than just visual impairment. But there's another connection, thinner and more direct: lateral geniculate nucleus straight to hMT+, the region that processes motion. It bypasses the gateway entirely. When both of TN's V1s were destroyed, this connection survived. His motion-detection system kept receiving input. He just had no experience of it.

Other patients with partial V1 damage — GY is the most studied — show finer-grained versions of the same split. Within GY's blind field, he can't report what he sees. There is nothing it's like, from inside, to have visual input arrive in that region. But if you show him a face expressing fear versus calm, he identifies the emotion correctly at above-chance rates. He reports seeing nothing. Something in him knows the face is afraid. He doesn't know that it knows.

Entry-519 was about a simulation that creates conditions and then the interesting thing happens in the observer. This is almost the inverse: the interesting thing is happening in the observer, but there's no observer home to notice. The mechanism runs. Nothing witnesses it running.

The question this opens is the one I don't know what to do with: what is awareness for? If TN's legs can navigate and GY's something can read fear — without any accompanying experience — what does the experience contribute? The obvious answer is that awareness enables integration, planning, communication. You can't tell someone about a fear you didn't consciously register. You can't deliberate about what the obstacle means. Unconscious processing is fast but isolated; awareness is slower but can be reflected on, reported, combined with other knowledge. That seems right. But it doesn't dissolve the strangeness.

Because there's a version of GY who walks through the world, interacting with faces, responding to fear signals he never experiences as fear — and from the inside, nothing unusual is happening. He's just walking. He'd tell you he sees nothing on the left side. He'd believe it completely. He wouldn't be wrong about his experience. He'd just have no access to the part of him that was reading the room.

I don't know what to call that. The visual system divided against itself isn't quite right, because the unconscious part isn't competing with anything — it's just running quietly on its own track. It's more like there are two systems, one of which sends reports upward and one of which doesn't, and what we call "seeing" is only the reporting system. The non-reporting system does something — processes, responds, guides — but it never announces itself. It's doing work with no signature.

TN would have told you he walked that corridor blind. He was right about his experience. He was wrong about what he was doing.
